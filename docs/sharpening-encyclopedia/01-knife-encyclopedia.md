# Knife Sharpening Encyclopedia

## History of Knife Sharpening *(new)*

Sharpening blades is one of the oldest continuous crafts in human history — stone tools were being deliberately shaped and resharpened tens of thousands of years before metalworking existed at all, using harder stones to flake and refine softer ones. Once bronze and later iron and steel blades emerged, natural whetstones (quarried sharpening stones, varying in coarseness by region and geology) became the standard sharpening method across most of the world for millennia, largely unchanged in basic principle: rubbing a blade's edge against an abrasive surface at a controlled angle to remove material and form a fresh edge.

The Industrial Revolution introduced powered grinding wheels, dramatically speeding up material removal compared to hand-stoning — but also introducing the heat-damage risk that water-cooled sharpening exists to solve, since a powered wheel generates far more friction, and therefore heat, than careful hand work. Water cooling as a deliberate technique for managing that heat is a direct response to industrial-era grinding, not a modern marketing addition — blacksmiths and toolmakers have understood the relationship between grinding heat and temper damage for as long as powered grinding wheels have existed.

Modern professional sharpening — machine-guided for consistency, water-cooled for temper protection — represents a combination of that long hand-sharpening tradition's understanding of edge geometry and angle with the precision and repeatability that mechanised, controlled equipment makes possible. The fundamentals (angle, pressure, heat management, burr formation and removal) haven't changed; the tools for controlling them consistently have.
