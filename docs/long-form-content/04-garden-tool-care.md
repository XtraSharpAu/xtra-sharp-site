## Medium (~400 words)

Garden tools take a beating that most other blades don't — dirt, sap, moisture, and repeated impact — which makes routine care as important as the occasional professional sharpen.

**Secateurs and pruning shears:** Clean sap and plant residue off after each use (a build-up affects both the pivot action and the edge). Keep the pivot bolt finger-tight — not loose enough to wobble, not so tight the blades bind. Dry thoroughly before storing to prevent rust forming at the pivot or along the edge.

**Hedge shears:** Same cleaning principle, with extra attention to the longer blade surface where sap residue tends to build up unevenly. Check the pivot tension periodically — a shear that's developed play at the pivot will feel less precise even with a perfectly sharp edge.

**Axes and tomahawks:** Wipe the head down after use, especially in damp conditions, to prevent surface rust. Check the handle for cracks or looseness — a sharp edge on a compromised handle is a safety issue, not just a performance one. Store with the edge protected (a sheath or cover) to avoid incidental damage.

**Across all tool types, the sharpening trigger is the same:** when cutting starts to feel like more effort than it should — crushing rather than slicing, or requiring noticeably more hand pressure than you remember needing — that's the point where cleaning and maintenance alone won't fix it, and professional sharpening is due.

**Why sharpness matters for plant health, not just convenience:** a sharp blade makes a single, clean cut through plant tissue. A blunt one crushes and tears, damaging a wider area of tissue around the cut than the cut itself, which heals more slowly and offers more entry points for disease — a real consideration for anyone pruning roses or fruit trees.

## Long (~580 words)

Garden tools live a harder life than most blades in the house — exposed to dirt, moisture, sap, and repeated impact against plant material — which is exactly why routine care matters as much for garden tools as the occasional professional sharpen.

### Secateurs & Pruning Shears

These see the most frequent use of any garden cutting tool for most gardeners, and their maintenance needs reflect that:

- Clean sap and plant residue off after every use — left in place, it builds up at the pivot and along the blade, affecting both action and edge.
- Keep the pivot bolt at the right tension: finger-tight is the usual guide — loose enough to move freely, tight enough that the blades don't wobble against each other.
- Dry thoroughly before storing. Moisture left on the blade, especially combined with plant sap residue, accelerates rust formation.

### Hedge Shears

Longer blades mean sap and residue tend to build up unevenly along the cutting surface, so a thorough wipe-down after use matters more here than it might seem. Periodically check pivot tension — shears that have developed play at the pivot feel imprecise even when the edge itself is freshly sharpened, which can be mistaken for a sharpening issue when it's actually a mechanical one.

### Axes & Tomahawks

Different failure modes than pivoted tools: wipe the head down after use, particularly in damp conditions, since surface rust forms faster on axe heads than most people expect. Check the handle regularly for cracks, splits, or looseness at the head — this is a genuine safety consideration, since a sharp edge on a compromised handle is more dangerous than a blunt one on a solid handle. Store with the edge covered or sheathed to prevent incidental damage in transit or storage.

### The Common Thread: Clean Cuts vs. Ragged Cuts

Across every tool type, the reason sharpness matters goes beyond convenience. A sharp blade makes a single, clean pass through plant tissue. A blunt one crushes and tears rather than slicing — damaging a wider area of cell tissue around the actual cut point than a sharp blade would. Plants heal clean cuts faster, and a smaller, cleaner wound offers fewer entry points for fungal and bacterial infection — a genuine concern for anyone pruning roses, fruit trees, or other disease-susceptible plants.

### Recognising When It's Time to Sharpen

The trigger is consistent across tool types: when cutting starts requiring noticeably more hand pressure than it used to, or the tool crushes/tears rather than cleanly slicing through material it used to handle easily, routine cleaning and oiling won't fix that — it's time for a professional sharpen.

### Timing It Right

The best time to get garden tools sharpened is before the growing season ramps up, not partway through when you're relying on them daily and discover mid-task that they're not performing.
