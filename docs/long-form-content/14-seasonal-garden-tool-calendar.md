# A Seasonal Garden Tool Maintenance Calendar (~1550 words)

Garden tools don't dull at a constant rate throughout the year — they see concentrated heavy use during growing and pruning seasons and comparative idle time over winter. A maintenance calendar built around that real usage pattern, rather than a fixed arbitrary schedule, gets more out of both your tools and your sharpening budget.

*(This is a scheduling/planning guide. For the underlying care principles per tool type, see `docs/long-form-content/04-garden-tool-care.md`; this guide is about timing, not technique.)*

## Why Timing Matters More for Garden Tools Than Indoor Blades

A kitchen knife sees roughly consistent use year-round. Garden tools don't — secateurs and hedge shears might be in near-daily use through spring and summer pruning and hedging season, then largely idle over winter. Sharpening on a fixed calendar interval regardless of actual use either wastes money sharpening tools that have barely been touched, or leaves tools running dull through their heaviest-use period because the "next scheduled sharpen" doesn't line up with when the tool actually needed it.

## Southern Hemisphere Seasonal Calendar

*(Timing below follows general Australian seasonal patterns — adjust against your own region's actual climate and your specific plants' growth patterns, which can vary meaningfully across Australia.)*

### Late Winter / Early Spring (Aug–Sep): Pre-Season Sharpen

This is the single most important sharpening window of the year for most home gardeners. Getting secateurs, hedge shears, and any pruning tools sharpened before the growing season ramps up means they're ready exactly when demand for them peaks, rather than discovering mid-pruning-session that they're not performing. This is also a sensible time to inspect handles and pivots for any winter storage issues (rust, stiffness) before relying on the tools daily.

### Spring (Sep–Nov): Active Monitoring, Not Necessarily Re-Sharpening

During peak use, this is the period to watch for the early signs covered in `docs/customer-education-pack/when-to-sharpen.md` — crushing rather than clean cuts, more hand effort required than at the season's start. A tool sharpened properly going into spring generally shouldn't need another full sharpen mid-season for typical home use, but heavy users (larger properties, extensive hedging) may find a mid-season touch-up worthwhile if signs of dulling show up before the season's out.

### Summer (Dec–Feb): Continued Use, Watch for Heat and Sap Buildup

Summer growth on many plants means continued secateur and shear use. This is also when sap and resin buildup tends to be most noticeable on blades (many plants' sap flow and cutting residue are more pronounced in warmer months) — cleaning after use matters even more during this period than in cooler months, since buildup left on a warm blade can be more stubborn to remove later.

### Autumn (Mar–May): End-of-Season Sharpen Before Storage

This is the second key sharpening window, and one that's easy to overlook: sharpening tools before they go into off-season storage means they come out of storage ready to use next spring, rather than needing attention before that first pre-season job. It also means any damage or wear accumulated over the growing season is addressed while it's fresh, rather than forgotten about over winter and rediscovered as a surprise next spring.

### Winter (Jun–Aug): Storage and Axe/Tomahawk Season

With secateurs and shears largely idle, winter is a sensible window for axe and tomahawk sharpening if firewood-cutting or similar winter tasks are part of your routine — these tools often see their heaviest use in exactly the season garden pruning tools are quietest. It's also the right time to do a proper storage check on idle tools: confirming they're dry, rust-free, and edge-protected for their idle months (see `docs/customer-education-pack/storage.md`).

## Building Your Own Calendar Around This Framework

The seasons above are a framework, not a rule — your actual calendar should reflect your real usage:

1. **Note when each tool sees its heaviest use** — for most home gardeners, secateurs and hedge shears peak through spring and into summer; axes may peak in a completely different season depending on why you use one (firewood prep is often a cooler-months task).
2. **Schedule the pre-heavy-use sharpen accordingly** — the goal is always "sharpened just before the tool's busiest period," not a fixed calendar date that might not line up with when you actually need it most.
3. **Add an end-of-heavy-use sharpen before storage**, so tools go into their idle period in good condition rather than needing attention rediscovered next time they're picked up.
4. **Watch for genuine mid-season signs of dulling** (per `docs/customer-education-pack/when-to-sharpen.md`) rather than assuming the two-sharpens-a-year framework is rigid — heavy users may need a mid-season touch-up, light users may find one annual sharpen is genuinely sufficient.

## Batching Multiple Tools Together

If you're sharpening secateurs, hedge shears, and other garden tools on a similar seasonal cycle, sending them in together (rather than as each individually seems to need it) is more efficient — see `docs/long-form-content/06-mail-in-sharpening-tips.md`'s batching advice, which applies to drop-off scheduling too. A single pre-spring visit covering your whole garden tool kit is simpler to plan around than several separate trips spread across the season.

## What This Calendar Doesn't Cover

This is about routine maintenance timing for tools in otherwise reasonable condition. A tool that's been significantly neglected (heavy rust, years without sharpening, handle damage) is a restoration job rather than a routine seasonal sharpen — see the knife-focused version of this distinction in `docs/long-form-content/11-restoring-a-neglected-knife.md`, which applies the same logic (a genuine assessment first, rather than assuming a routine sharpen will fully address years of neglect).

## The Bottom Line

Garden tools have a real, predictable seasonal usage pattern, and a maintenance calendar built around that pattern — sharpened just before each tool's heaviest-use period, and again before it goes into off-season storage — gets more genuine value out of both the tools and the sharpening budget than either a fixed arbitrary schedule or purely reactive sharpening once something's already noticeably struggling.
