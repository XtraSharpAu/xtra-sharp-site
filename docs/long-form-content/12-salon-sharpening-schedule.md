# Setting Up a Salon Sharpening Schedule (~1550 words)

For an individual customer, sharpening is something you think about when scissors start feeling dull. For a salon running multiple stylists through multiple pairs of shears every single day, that reactive approach doesn't really work — by the time dullness is obvious enough for someone to notice and mention it, it's usually already been affecting cut quality for a while. A proper sharpening schedule turns this from an occasional scramble into a predictable, budgeted part of running the business.

## Why Salons Specifically Need a Different Approach to Individual Customers

A home user might sharpen one pair of scissors every year or two. A working salon is putting far more cutting hours on each pair of shears in a single month than a home user racks up in years — which means the "when did it last get sharpened" question needs a genuinely different answer for a business than for an individual, and needs to be tracked deliberately rather than left to memory or a stylist happening to mention it.

There's also a business-quality dimension that doesn't apply to home use in the same way: every stylist's cut quality reflects on the salon's reputation, and a stylist quietly working around a scissors that's a bit past its best (pushing rather than cutting cleanly, needing extra passes) is a hidden cost that shows up in client experience and staff frustration before it ever shows up as an obvious complaint.

## Building the Actual Schedule

**Step 1: Inventory what you have.** List every pair of scissors in active use across the salon — not just each stylist's primary shears, but backups, thinning scissors, and anything used across multiple stylists. Note the edge type (bevel or convex) for each, since this affects both cost and how frequently convex-edge premium shears in particular tend to need attention given their finer, more precise edge geometry.

**Step 2: Estimate realistic usage.** A stylist doing back-to-back cuts most working days puts meaningfully more wear on their primary shears than a part-time stylist, or than backup pairs kept for occasional use. A one-size-fits-all schedule across every pair in the salon usually means some pairs are sharpened more often than they need (wasted cost) while others go too long between sharpens (the exact problem the schedule is meant to solve).

**Step 3: Set a baseline interval, then adjust per pair.** A reasonable starting point for a primary pair in daily heavy use is every 2–3 months; less frequently used backups might reasonably stretch to 6 months. These aren't fixed rules — they're a starting point to refine based on what stylists actually report once the schedule is running (see Step 5).

**Step 4: Batch the schedule, don't stagger it randomly.** Sending scissors in for sharpening in a coordinated batch — say, all primary shears together on the same cycle — is more efficient than trickling individual pairs in whenever someone happens to notice dullness, and makes it easier for both the salon and the sharpening service to plan around (see `docs/business-systems-pack/02-workflow-documentation.md`'s Drop-Off and Mail-In workflows for how batch bookings are handled operationally on the sharpening side).

**Step 5: Ask stylists directly, and adjust the schedule based on real feedback.** The people actually using the scissors every day are the best source of information on whether the interval is right — if a stylist says their primary shears are starting to feel like work again before the next scheduled sharpen, that pair's interval should shorten; if a backup pair genuinely never gets close to needing it within its scheduled window, that interval can stretch.

## Coordinating Around Salon Downtime

The practical challenge with a shared tool (scissors that a stylist needs every single working day) is coordinating sharpening around when the salon can actually spare them. A few approaches:

- **Stagger by stylist, not all-at-once**, if the salon can't function with every primary pair out simultaneously — this spreads the "down" period across the roster rather than concentrating it.
- **Maintain backup shears** specifically to rotate through while primary pairs are out — this is standard practice at salons with an established sharpening schedule, and the backup pairs benefit from the same batch scheduling.
- **Choose a consistently quiet period** (a slow weekday, or a planned closure day if the salon has one) for whichever portion of the kit can go out together.
- **Communicate turnaround honestly with the sharpening service**, and plan around real turnaround windows (typically same day/next day for drop-off) rather than assuming same-day availability for a large batch without checking first.

## Budgeting for a Sharpening Schedule

Once a schedule is established, sharpening becomes a predictable line item rather than an occasional surprise cost. For budgeting purposes:

- Calculate the annual cost per pair based on the chosen interval (e.g. a primary pair sharpened every 2–3 months is roughly 4–6 sharpens a year).
- Multiply across the full inventory from Step 1, accounting for the different intervals set per pair.
- Factor in that convex-edge premium shears cost more per sharpen than bevel-edge scissors (see `docs/long-form-content/02-scissors-sharpening.md` for the full explanation of why), so a salon's primary shears — often convex — represent a larger share of the annual sharpening budget than backup or thinning scissors.
- Consider whether batch/commercial pricing arrangements are available (see `docs/business-systems-pack/07-business-growth-systems.md`'s upsell/cross-sell notes on batch pricing for businesses) — a standing arrangement can be more predictable and sometimes more cost-effective than ad hoc individual bookings.

## Signs the Schedule Needs Adjusting

- **Stylists mentioning scissors feel "off" before the scheduled sharpen** — the interval for that pair is too long.
- **No noticeable difference in cutting feel across the whole scheduled interval** — the interval could potentially stretch slightly, saving cost, though this should be balanced against the fact that gradual dulling is often not consciously noticed until it's fairly advanced.
- **Inconsistent results between stylists using similarly-scheduled pairs** — may indicate one stylist's actual usage pattern differs enough from the schedule's assumption that their pairs need their own adjusted interval.

## Why This Is Worth the Effort

A salon that treats sharpening reactively — waiting until a stylist actively complains — is always running at least some of its shears in a degraded state without realising it, since gradual dulling isn't something people reliably notice in the moment. A proper schedule, once set up, removes that blind spot: cutting quality stays consistent across the whole team, the cost becomes predictable rather than a surprise, and stylists aren't quietly compensating for a tool that isn't performing the way it should.

## The Bottom Line

Setting up a sharpening schedule for a salon is a one-time investment of effort — inventorying the kit, estimating usage, setting initial intervals, and coordinating around downtime — that pays off as reduced hidden cost (in cut quality and stylist frustration) and a predictable, budgeted maintenance expense instead of an occasional scramble when someone finally notices their shears have been struggling for weeks.
