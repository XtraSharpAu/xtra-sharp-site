# Comparing Clipper Blade Sizes and When to Use Each (~1550 words)

Clipper blade numbering can be genuinely confusing if you're not immersed in grooming equipment day to day — a "size 10" and a "wide" blade aren't referring to the same kind of measurement, and picking the wrong blade for a job affects both the result and how hard the sharpened blade has to work. This guide covers what the common blade categories actually mean and when each is the right choice, as background for understanding what you're sending in for sharpening and why.

## Blade Length Matters as Much as Number

Clipper blades are generally categorised two ways at once: a size number (which relates to how short the resulting coat length is — a system that varies somewhat between manufacturers) and a width category (standard/A5-compatible, wide, or large-animal), which is about the physical size of the blade itself and what body area or animal size it's designed for.

## A5 (Standard-Width) Blades

The most common category for everyday grooming — A5-compatible blades fit the standard clipper body size used across most home and professional grooming clippers. Within A5, size numbers determine cut length: lower numbers leave more coat length, higher numbers cut closer. A5 blades are the right choice for general body grooming on cats, dogs, and most standard grooming work where the animal's size doesn't demand a wider blade for efficiency.

**Sharpening consideration**: A5 blades see the most use of any category for most groomers, simply by volume of jobs, so they're typically the first blades to show wear in a working kit and benefit most from being on a consistent sharpening rotation rather than sharpened reactively.

## Wide Blades

Wider than standard A5 blades, these cover more surface area per pass — genuinely useful for larger dogs or bigger jobs where efficiency matters (covering a large body area in fewer passes), but less practical for detail work around faces, paws, or tight areas where a narrower blade offers more control.

**When to reach for wide over A5**: larger breeds with substantial coat to clear, or any job where speed on large flat body areas matters more than fine control. Most working groomers keep both in rotation rather than choosing one exclusively.

**Sharpening consideration**: the wider cutting edge means slightly more surface to maintain evenly during sharpening — alignment across a longer blade edge matters just as much as on A5 blades, arguably more, since any unevenness has more length to show up across.

## Large-Animal Blades

Built for horses, livestock, and other large animals with thicker, coarser coats than typical companion-animal grooming. These are heavier-duty blades designed to handle tougher material without dulling as quickly as a standard blade would under the same conditions — but "designed to handle tougher material" doesn't mean "never needs sharpening," and large-animal blades still require the same attention to edge, alignment, and tension as any other clipper blade category.

**Sharpening consideration**: given the tougher material they're regularly cutting through, large-animal blades can be more prone to nicks from incidental contact with debris in a coat (dirt, burrs) than blades used on typically cleaner companion-animal coats — worth mentioning any known rough use when sending these in, since it affects the assessment.

## Choosing the Right Blade for the Job, Not Just What's On Hand

A common inefficiency in grooming work is reaching for whatever blade happens to be on the clipper rather than the blade actually suited to the job — which both produces a worse result and works the blade harder than necessary (a wide blade forced into detail work around a face, for instance, isn't just imprecise, it's also more likely to catch or drag in a way a properly-suited detail blade wouldn't).

Matching blade to job isn't just a quality-of-cut question — it also affects how quickly a given blade dulls. A blade consistently used outside its intended application (a standard A5 pushed through consistently tough, large-animal-grade coat, for example) will generally need more frequent sharpening than one used as intended.

## How Blade Category Affects Sharpening Approach

While the fundamental machine-guided sharpening process (angle, alignment, tension, tracking) applies across every blade category, there are real differences in what's being managed:

- **A5 blades**: the most standardised category, with well-understood angle and alignment expectations.
- **Wide blades**: same core process, applied across a longer cutting edge, where consistency along that length matters proportionally more.
- **Large-animal blades**: heavier-duty construction, sometimes with more wear/damage to address given the tougher material they regularly handle, but the same core alignment and tension principles apply.

## Building a Sensible Blade Kit

For a working groomer, a sensible kit generally includes multiple A5 blades in different size numbers for different coat lengths, at least one wide blade for larger jobs, and large-animal blades if the client base includes appropriate animals. Having backups in rotation — rather than a single blade per category — means sharpening can happen on a schedule (see `docs/long-form-content/12-salon-sharpening-schedule.md`'s scheduling logic, which applies just as well to grooming businesses as salons) without ever being without a usable blade mid-week.

## Recognising When a Blade Category Isn't Right for Your Work

If you're consistently finding a particular blade category needs sharpening far more often than others in your kit, it's worth asking whether it's being used outside its intended application, rather than assuming the blade itself is simply lower quality or the sharpening isn't holding. A standard A5 blade being pushed through large-animal-grade coat regularly, for instance, will show wear faster than the same blade used as intended — the fix there is adding a proper large-animal blade to the kit, not just sharpening the A5 more often.

## The Bottom Line

Clipper blade categories exist because different jobs genuinely need different tools — A5 for standard grooming, wide for efficient coverage of larger areas, large-animal for tougher coats on bigger animals. Using the right blade for the job produces a better result, puts less unnecessary strain on the blade, and means your sharpening schedule reflects genuine wear from appropriate use rather than compensating for a blade being asked to do a job it wasn't built for.
