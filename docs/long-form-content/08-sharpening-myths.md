# Sharpening Myths, Debunked

## Short (~180 words)

**"Expensive knives never need sharpening."** False — every blade dulls with use, regardless of price; higher-quality steel typically holds an edge longer, not indefinitely. **"A honing steel sharpens your knife."** It doesn't — honing realigns an existing edge, it doesn't restore a genuinely dull one. **"Sharper is more dangerous."** Actually the opposite in practice — dull blades require more force and are more likely to slip, causing more injuries than sharp ones. **"All sharpening methods are basically the same."** Not true — dry-grinding generates heat that can damage steel temper, while water-cooled sharpening avoids this. **"Ceramic/glass cutting boards don't affect knife edges."** They do — hard surfaces dull an edge far faster than wood or plastic. **"Convex and bevel scissors sharpening are the same process."** They're genuinely different techniques, which is why they're often priced differently.

## Medium (~420 words)

**Myth: "Expensive knives never need sharpening."**
Every blade dulls with use — that's simple physics, regardless of price. What higher-quality steel typically buys you is better edge retention (it stays sharp longer between sharpens), not immunity from ever needing it.

**Myth: "Running my knife over a honing steel is sharpening it."**
Honing and sharpening are different processes. Honing realigns the microscopic edge that bends slightly out of true with normal use — it doesn't remove metal or restore a genuinely dull blade. Honing extends the life of a sharp edge; it can't create one from scratch.

**Myth: "A sharper knife is more dangerous."**
Counterintuitively, the opposite tends to be true in practice. A dull blade requires more force to cut through material, and that extra force is exactly what makes it more likely to slip — which is how a lot of kitchen cuts actually happen. A sharp blade does the cutting with less pressure and more control.

**Myth: "All sharpening methods produce the same result."**
Not true. Fast, dry-grinding methods generate significant heat through friction, which can affect a blade's factory temper if not carefully managed. Water-cooled sharpening keeps the blade's temperature down throughout the process specifically to avoid this — the visible sharpness might look similar right after either method, but edge-holding ability and resistance to chipping can differ meaningfully.

**Myth: "The cutting board material doesn't matter, as long as it's flat."**
It matters a lot. Wood and plastic cutting boards give slightly under the blade, which is kind to the edge. Glass, stone, and ceramic boards are hard, unforgiving surfaces that dull an edge significantly faster — every cut is effectively grinding the blade against the board.

**Myth: "Bevel and convex scissors sharpening are basically the same job."**
They're genuinely different techniques. Bevel is a single flat-ground angle; convex is a rounded, hollow-ground curve that has to be maintained evenly along the entire blade with no flat reference surface to grind against. This is why convex sharpening typically costs more — it's not an arbitrary premium, it reflects real additional time and precision.

## Long (~560 words)

Sharpening has more than its fair share of folk wisdom floating around — some of it harmless, some of it genuinely leads people to treat their tools worse than they should. Here's what's actually true.

### "Expensive knives don't need sharpening"

Every blade, regardless of price, dulls with normal use — that's a simple consequence of cutting through material, not a manufacturing flaw. What a higher price generally buys you is steel with better edge retention properties, meaning it holds a sharp edge for longer between sharpens. It doesn't buy you a blade that never needs the process at all.

### "Honing is the same as sharpening"

These are genuinely different actions. A honing steel realigns the microscopic edge — the very thin, flexible line of metal at a blade's edge — which bends slightly out of true through normal use. A few strokes on a honing steel straightens that edge back into alignment, restoring a noticeable amount of cutting performance without removing any metal. Sharpening, by contrast, actually grinds the edge to remove metal and form a fresh cutting surface. Honing is genuinely useful maintenance between sharpens — but it can't create sharpness that isn't there to begin with, only maintain sharpness that already exists.

### "A sharper blade is a more dangerous blade"

This one runs backward from how injuries actually tend to happen. A dull blade requires significantly more downward and forward force to cut through material, and that extra force is unpredictable — it's exactly the situation where a blade is more likely to slip off whatever you're cutting and onto a hand or finger. A sharp blade cuts with controlled, minimal pressure, which is both safer and easier to manage.

### "All sharpening methods give the same result"

This is one of the more consequential myths, because it affects a real purchasing decision. Fast, dry-grinding sharpening methods generate substantial heat through friction against the blade. Without careful heat management, this heat can affect the blade's factory temper — the heat-treatment process that balances a blade's hardness (edge-holding ability) against its toughness (resistance to chipping). Water-cooled sharpening exists specifically to avoid this, keeping the blade at a safe temperature throughout grinding. Two blades can look equally sharp straight off two different sharpening processes, and still perform very differently over the following weeks of actual use.

### "Cutting board material doesn't really matter"

It does, and the difference is significant. Wood and plastic cutting boards give slightly under the pressure of a blade, which is gentle on the edge. Glass, stone, and ceramic boards are hard, unyielding surfaces — every single cut is effectively grinding the blade's edge against the board, dulling it far faster than a softer surface would.

### "Bevel and convex scissors sharpening are basically interchangeable in terms of effort"

They're genuinely different techniques requiring different skill and time. A bevel edge is ground at a single flat angle — a well-understood, relatively efficient process. A convex edge is hollow-ground into a continuous curve with no flat reference surface, requiring the entire curve to be maintained evenly along the full blade length by feel and control rather than against a fixed angle guide. The price difference between bevel and convex sharpening reflects genuinely different amounts of skill and time, not an arbitrary premium.

### The Bottom Line

A lot of sharpening folklore sounds plausible but doesn't hold up against how blades, steel, and sharpening processes actually work. Understanding the real mechanics behind sharpness — and why it fades — makes it easier to take care of your tools properly, and to know what you're actually paying for when you get them professionally sharpened.
