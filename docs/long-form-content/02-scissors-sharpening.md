## Medium (~380 words)

Unlike a knife, which is a single blade with one edge, scissors are a two-blade system — which is why sharpening them properly involves more than grinding an edge.

**Angle and edge.** Each blade needs its cutting edge restored at the correct angle, matched to the scissors' original design.

**Ride line.** This is how the two blades meet and glide against each other as the scissors close — if it's off, even a perfectly sharp pair will feel like it's not quite biting cleanly.

**Tension.** The pivot screw tension affects how the blades meet along their length as they close. Too loose and the scissors feel floppy and don't cut cleanly at the tip; too tight and they're hard to operate and wear faster.

**Alignment.** The blades need to track true against each other along their full length, not just at one point.

A machine-guided sharpening process addresses all four together — which is why proper scissors sharpening restores overall cutting performance, not just edge sharpness on its own.

Bevel and convex are the two main edge types. Bevel is a single flat-ground angle — standard on most household, sewing, and general grooming scissors. Convex is a rounded, hollow-ground edge, standard on premium hairdressing shears, producing an especially smooth, clean cut but taking considerably more time and precision to sharpen correctly, which is reflected in the price difference.

## Long (~600 words)

A pair of scissors is a small mechanical system, not just two sharp blades — and that's the key to understanding why proper scissors sharpening is a genuinely different process from knife sharpening, not just a smaller-scale version of it.

### Four Things That Have to Be Right Together

**Edge angle.** Each blade's cutting edge is ground at a specific angle, matched to how that type of scissors is designed to cut. Get this wrong and the scissors will feel sharp under a fingertip test but perform poorly on the material they're meant to cut.

**Ride line.** As scissors close, the two blades slide against each other along a shared contact line — the ride line. If this isn't correct, the blades can gap slightly as they close, which lets material (hair, fabric, paper) slip through instead of being cleanly sheared.

**Tension.** Set at the pivot screw, tension controls how firmly the two blades press against each other through the cutting motion. Too loose, and the scissors feel "floppy," with poor cutting especially near the tips. Too tight, and they're stiff to operate, tire the hand faster, and wear down more quickly.

**Alignment.** The blades need to track true against each other consistently along their whole length — not just be correctly positioned at the pivot.

A sharpening process that only addresses the edge and ignores the other three will produce scissors that are technically sharper but don't actually cut any better — sometimes worse, if tension or alignment has shifted during the process. This is why professional scissors sharpening is described as machine-guided: it's a controlled process addressing angle, ride line, tension and alignment as one connected system.

### Bevel vs. Convex

The two main edge types found on scissors require genuinely different sharpening approaches:

- **Bevel edges** are ground with a single flat angle — the standard on most household, sewing, craft, and general-purpose grooming scissors. They're reliable and can be sharpened efficiently while still doing the job properly.
- **Convex edges** are hollow-ground into a smooth, continuous curve rather than a flat angle — the standard on premium hairdressing shears, where the quality of every cut directly affects the finished result. Convex sharpening takes considerably longer and more precision, because there's no flat reference surface to grind against — the entire curve has to be maintained evenly along the full blade length. This is directly reflected in pricing.

### Who Needs What

- **Hairdressers and barbers** typically use convex-edge shears for their primary cutting scissors, given the smoothness of cut that edge type provides.
- **Groomers** use a mix, with premium shears often convex and everyday/thinning scissors more commonly bevel.
- **Sewing, craft, and household scissors** are almost always bevel, and don't need convex-level precision for their typical use.
