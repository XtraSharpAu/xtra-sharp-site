# Signs Your Scissors Need Sharpening (Before They Damage What You're Cutting)

Scissors don't usually go from sharp to unusable overnight — they decline gradually, which is exactly why so many people keep using a pair well past the point they should have been sharpened. Here's what to watch for.

## For Hairdressers and Barbers

- **Pushing hair instead of cutting it** — you'll feel the scissors bending or displacing the hair slightly before the blades actually bite through, rather than a single clean pass.
- **Uneven ends on a straight cut** — a duller or misaligned blade can leave micro-snags that show up as unevenness once the client's hair dries and settles.
- **Increased hand fatigue during a cut** — if you're gripping harder or feeling more strain by the end of a day than you used to, tension or sharpness may have drifted.
- **Audible difference in the cut** — a sharp pair of shears has a distinct, clean sound; a dulling pair often sounds duller and "softer" too.

## For Groomers

- **Pulling instead of gliding** through fur, especially on thicker or matted coats
- **The animal reacting more than usual** to being groomed — discomfort from a dragging blade is a real welfare consideration, not just a convenience issue
- **Visibly uneven results** on a finish that should be clean and consistent

## For Sewing and Craft Use

- **Fabric bunching or folding into the blades** instead of being cut cleanly through — a strong sign the edge has lost its bite
- **Needing multiple passes** on material that used to cut in one clean stroke

## Beyond the Edge: Tension and Alignment

Scissors sharpening isn't only about the edge. If the ride line (how the two blades meet and glide) or the tension is off, scissors can feel "loose" or "floppy" — cutting inconsistently even if the edge itself is technically fine. This is why proper scissors sharpening is a machine-guided process addressing angle, ride line, tension and alignment together, not just running the edge over a stone.

## Bevel or Convex — Both Need Attention

Whether your scissors have a standard bevel edge or a premium convex edge, the same warning signs apply — the difference is in what sharpening technique is needed to restore them correctly (see our post on bevel vs. convex sharpening for the full breakdown).
