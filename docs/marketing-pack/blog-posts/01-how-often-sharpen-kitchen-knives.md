# How Often Should You Sharpen Your Kitchen Knives?

If you've ever caught yourself sawing through a tomato instead of slicing it, or pressing harder than you used to just to get through an onion, your knife is probably telling you something: it's due for a sharpen.

## The Signs Before You Even Think About It

Most people wait far too long between sharpenings, mostly because dullness creeps up slowly rather than happening overnight. A few reliable signs it's time:

- The blade slides off the skin of a tomato instead of biting in
- You're using more pressure than you remember needing
- The edge visibly reflects light in a thin white line when you look straight down the blade (a sharp edge is too thin to catch light this way)
- Cutting feels more like crushing than slicing, especially on soft foods

## So, How Often — Really?

There's no single answer that fits every kitchen, because it depends entirely on how the knife is used:

- **Home cooks, a few meals a week**: most kitchen knives hold a usable edge for 3–6 months before a professional sharpen is worth it, especially if you're doing some honing in between (see below).
- **Keen home cooks, daily use**: closer to every 2–3 months.
- **Commercial kitchens, restaurants, butchers**: often weekly or fortnightly for the knives in daily heavy use — this is why batch sharpening arrangements exist for businesses, rather than sending knives in one at a time as they go blunt.

## Honing Isn't Sharpening

A common mix-up: running your knife across a honing steel isn't sharpening it — it's straightening the edge between proper sharpens. Honing can extend the life of an edge day-to-day, but it doesn't remove metal or restore a genuinely dull blade the way professional sharpening does. Think of honing as maintenance, and sharpening as the actual restoration.

## Why Water-Cooled Sharpening Matters

Not all sharpening is equal. Dry grinding — the fast, high-friction method some sharpening tools and machines use — generates enough heat to affect the temper of the steel, which can leave a blade that looks sharp but doesn't hold its edge well or chips more easily. Water-cooled sharpening keeps the blade's temperature down throughout the process, protecting the steel and giving a cleaner, longer-lasting edge.

A knife that's sharpened regularly is safer to use (less pressure means less chance of slipping), less frustrating in the kitchen, and easier on the steel long-term than one that's left to go badly dull between occasional sharpens.
