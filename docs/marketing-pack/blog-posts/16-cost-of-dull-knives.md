# The Real Cost of Cooking with Dull Knives

A dull knife feels like a minor inconvenience — until you look at what it actually costs in the kitchen, in both safety and time.

## The Safety Problem Is Backwards From What Most People Assume

Most people assume a sharp knife is the dangerous one. In practice, the opposite is usually true. A dull blade needs more downward pressure to cut through the same food, and more pressure means less control — the knife is more likely to slip off a curved surface (an onion, a tomato) than to bite in cleanly. A sharp blade under light, controlled pressure behaves predictably. A dull one under heavy pressure doesn't.

## Time Adds Up Faster Than It Feels

Cutting with a dull edge takes visibly longer per item — more sawing motions, more repositioning, more food that tears rather than slices cleanly. Multiply that across every meal prepped in a week, and the extra time isn't trivial. For commercial kitchens prepping in volume, this compounds into a real labour cost, not just an inconvenience.

## Food Quality and Presentation Suffer

A clean cut and a torn cut behave differently once cooked. Torn or crushed surfaces on vegetables and proteins can affect texture, browning, and how evenly something cooks. For presentation-focused dishes, ragged cuts are visibly worse than clean ones — this matters as much in a home kitchen plating for guests as it does commercially.

## It's Not Just the Blade — It's the Whole Task

A dull knife changes how people cook. Some cuts get avoided (thin slicing, fine dicing) because they're frustrating with a dull edge, which narrows what actually gets cooked. Others push through anyway with more force than the task needs, which is exactly the scenario described above under safety.

## The Fix Is Simple and Infrequent

None of this requires obsessive maintenance — most home kitchen knives only need professional sharpening every few months with regular honing in between. The cost of staying on top of it is small compared to the accumulated cost — in time, safety, and food quality — of letting an edge go dull for months or years at a stretch.
