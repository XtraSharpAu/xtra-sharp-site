# Knife Steel Hardness Explained — What Rockwell Ratings Mean for Sharpening

You'll sometimes see knife steel described with a Rockwell hardness number (HRC). Here's what that number actually means for how a knife sharpens and holds an edge.

## What Rockwell Hardness Measures

The Rockwell C scale measures how much a standardised point resists being pressed into the steel — in practice, a proxy for how hard the steel is. Most kitchen knife steel sits somewhere in the mid-to-high 50s to low 60s HRC. It's a material property of the steel itself, not a measure of sharpness — a soft steel and a hard steel can both be put to the same degree of sharpness, they just get there and stay there differently.

## Harder Steel Holds an Edge Longer

Higher-HRC steel generally resists wear better, so the edge stays sharp through more use before it needs attention again. This is why many premium Japanese-style knives run harder than typical Western kitchen knives — the trade-off is usually worth it for someone who wants longer stretches between sharpenings.

## Harder Steel Is Also More Brittle

The trade-off for wear resistance is toughness. Harder steel is more prone to chipping on hard contact — a bone, a frozen surface, a ceramic plate — where a softer steel might roll or dent instead of chip outright. This is part of why harder, more brittle blades usually come with more specific care instructions (no bones, no frozen food, no dishwasher, no cutting on ceramic or glass).

## Softer Steel Is Easier to Maintain at Home

Lower-HRC steel dulls faster under regular use but is more forgiving — it's less likely to chip on an awkward cut, and it's usually easier to bring back to a working edge with a home honing steel between professional sharpens. This is part of why a lot of everyday Western kitchen knives sit in a more moderate hardness range: it suits how most home cooks actually treat their knives.

## What This Means for Sharpening

The angle and process used during sharpening take the specific steel into account — a harder, more brittle edge is typically set at a slightly less acute angle than a softer one, trading a small amount of cutting performance for real resistance to chipping. This is one of the reasons a generic "one angle fits all" approach to sharpening isn't ideal, and why professional sharpening — where the person doing it can see and feel what a specific blade needs — tends to outperform a fixed-angle guide or gadget.

## The Bottom Line

Rockwell hardness isn't something you need to know off the top of your head to get good results — but understanding that harder steel trades edge retention for brittleness (and vice versa) explains a lot about why different knives need different care, and why the same sharpening approach doesn't suit every blade.
